## The Caricatures of Simplicity

When many people first encounter minimalism, they are greeted by an unrealistic aesthetic: stark white Scandinavian apartments, an ascetic wardrobe of identical black t-shirts, and social media influencers bragging about owning fewer than 50 items.

This is not minimalism — it is **aesthetic perfectionism**.

When minimalism degenerates into a set of rigid, moralistic rules, it ceases to be an instrument of liberation and becomes just another source of anxiety.

> "Beware of the minimalism that is merely consumerism in disguise."

## The Four Major Traps

### 1. Aesthetic Consumerism
The urge to throw away perfectly good possessions simply because they don't match a neutral color palette, only to buy expensive "minimalist-branded" replacements. True minimalism honors the utility of what you already own.

### 2. The 100-Item Dogma
Counting every fork, sock, and paperclip turns a liberating lifestyle into an obsessive accounting exercise. The right number of items depends on your family, your climate, and your craft.

### 3. Decluttering Addiction
The perpetual cycle of purging items, feeling a brief dopamine high from empty space, re-accumulating clutter, and purging again. Without addressing the inflow of consumption, decluttering is just rearranging noise.

### 4. Minimalist Guilt & Gatekeeping
Feeling guilty about keeping family heirlooms, musical instruments, books you love, or hobby tools. Minimalism is subtractive so that you can amplify what brings you joy.

## A Healthier Compass

Ask yourself: **Does this lifestyle serve me, or am I serving an aesthetic?** If getting rid of a tool makes your daily routine harder, it is not minimalism.
